Public Transport Usage in Australia 🇦🇺

How Australians Ride Trains, Trams, Buses and Ferries.

Public transport patronage means the number of trips people make on trains, trams (also called light rail), buses and ferries. Australia's big cities had been adding riders every year until the pandemic emptied them almost overnight.

Five years later, public transport recovery remains uneven across Australian cities. This page examines the national recovery, takes a closer look at Sydney and Melbourne, and explores how cheaper fares helped bring passengers back.

1 The Big Picture — How far has public transport recovered across Australia's eight capital cities since the pandemic?

Trips across the capitals fell to 862 million in 2021–2022, less than half their usual level. By 2024–2025 Perth had passed its old record, Brisbane and Canberra were back to normal, but Melbourne was still 18% short.

Patronage

One trip or boarding on a train, tram, bus or ferry. A journey with a change of vehicle can count as two.

Financial year

Australia's government year runs July to June, so "2024–2025" means July 2024 to June 2025.

2 Sydney: Recovery — How quickly did Sydney's riders return, and where did they go once the new Metro opened?

Sydney recorded just 9 million trips in August 2021, down from 63 million in a typical 2019 month. By 2025, ridership had recovered to 88% of 2019 (all Opal trips), with the new Metro shifting passenger flows to different stations.

Opal

Metro

Sydney’s driverless rail network, with five new stations serving the city centre and North Sydney, opened in August 2024.

The busiest place recovered the least. The city centre is the darkest square in every year, but its stations carried 227 million trips in 2019 and only 169 million in 2025 (75%), below the 82% across all Sydney stations (station entries, a different count from all Opal trips). Squares with a new Metro station did best: North Sydney–Crows Nest grew from 18 to 22 million trips (+24%), and Chatswood is back to its 2019 level.

Most stations are still short of 2019: together they carried 82% of their 2019 entries. The biggest drop is North Sydney (33%), whose riders moved to the new Victoria Cross Metro station next door. Most stations above 110% are on the Metro North West line, which opened in May 2019, so their 2019 count covers only part of the year.

Riders moved, they didn't vanish. In 2025 Central became Sydney's busiest station, overtaking Town Hall, which fell from 71 million trips in 2019 to 41 million.

Martin Place, which gained a Metro station in 2024, jumped into the top five with 21.6 million trips. Circular Quay had the biggest fall, from 5th to 9th (21.4 million to 13.5 million trips).

A break in a line, or a line that starts or stops part-way, means that station was outside the top 10 that year.

3 Melbourne: The City-Centre Effect — Why is the city centre recovering more slowly?

Victoria's trains, trams and buses carried 477 million trips in 2025, 81% of 2019. Its station data shows why the city centre matters. At the five busiest city-centre stations, including Flinders Street, Southern Cross and Melbourne Central, only 5–10% of peak-time boardings happen in the morning.

These are places people travel to for work, so when office workers stay home, these stations lose the most.

The further out, the more morning boardings. At the busiest city-centre stations, within 2 km of Flinders Street, only 5–10% of peak entries happen in the morning. Between 3 and 10 km out, the typical station is 61% morning, and beyond 10 km it is 71%, with Craigieburn at 82% and Werribee at 79%.

Where it happens: 27 of the 32 mostly-evening (blue) stations sit inside the 10 km ring, while gold morning stations stretch out along every line. People leave home from the suburbs and arrive in the city, so the city-centre stations depend on office workers coming back.

Weekday Entries by Time of Day

City stations are evening stations. Each row of the heatmap adds up to 100%. The city-centre stations at the top take most of their day's entries in the evening peak: 68% at Parliament, 64% at Flagstaff and 63% at Southern Cross. That is because people board there to go home after work.

Suburban stations are more even. Craigieburn, on the city's northern edge, is the opposite: 48% of its entries come in the morning peak and only 11% in the evening.

Regional trains came back first. They have been above their 2019 level in 32 of the 42 months since January 2023, averaging +19% in 2025 and +30% in the first half of 2026.

The lockdowns hit every mode equally. In 2020 trips fell 83–93% below 2019, and in 2022 every mode was still 29–43% down.

Every metropolitan mode is still below 2019. Trams are furthest behind (−23% in 2025), metro trains are closing the gap (from −24% in 2025 to −10% so far in 2026), and buses are closest (−5%).

4 What Brings Riders Back: The Price of a Ticket

On 5 August 2024, Queensland cut every public transport fare in the south-east to just 50 Australian cents. In the same December-to-July months a year later, South East Queensland carried 16% more trips and the gain held into a second year.

Every mode gained, and buses, the largest mode, had their busiest month in March 2026 with 13.5 million trips.

South East Queensland

Brisbane, the Gold Coast and the Sunshine Coast.

Every mode carried more people after fares fell to 50 cents. Overall, average monthly trips rose 16%, from 13.7 million to 16.0 million.

  • Who rode more: ferry and light rail grew fastest, which suggests cheap fares attracted optional trips, such as weekend outings, not only commuting.
  • Not proof of cause: The charts show changes following the fare cut. However, this does not mean the fare cut was the only cause. Other factors like population growth, may also have contributed to the increase in ridership.
  • Biggest gain in numbers: buses. Although buses grew the least in percentage terms (+12%), they added about 1.07 million extra trips a month, the most of any mode and almost half of the total increase.

Change in Monthly Trips from a Year Earlier: Queensland and Victoria

Queensland: cheap fares lifted travel, and the lift has held.

  • January – July 2025: +16% on average. Each month is compared with the same month on full fares, so this shows the effect of the fare cut.
  • From August 2025: +11% on average. Both years now had 50-cent fares, so this is growth on top of the cut. Leaving out March 2026, which is compared with the cyclone-hit March 2025, it is about +8%.

Victoria: free weekend travel made no visible difference.

  • During the offer (December 2025 – January 2026): about 0%. Trips were almost the same as a year earlier (+0.1% and −0.2%).
  • April 2026: +25%, but this came after the offer had ended, so it is not a free-travel effect.
  • Monthly totals mix weekday and weekend trips, so a small weekend-only effect could be hidden.
